01 · THE QUESTION

Do people differ by when, not just how much?

The easy way to sort electricity customers is by size: small, medium, large. It is also the least useful, because it says nothing about when demand lands on the grid. The question here is whether households fall into distinct timing patterns - a daytime shape, an evening shape, a flat shape - that survive once you remove the effect of sheer volume.

02 · THE DATA

A controlled, synthetic world

To ask the question cleanly, the data is generated, not measured. A generator draws each consumer from one of four hidden archetypes, then adds amplitude, timing jitter, weekday and weekend differences, and noise. That hidden label is set aside before any modelling and used only, at the very end, to check the answer.

03 · THE SHAPE OF A DAY

Divide out the size

Each consumer's 24 hourly values are scaled to sum to one, turning a load amount into a load shape. After that step, a household that uses ten times the energy but at the same hours has an identical curve. What is left to cluster on is pure timing.

The faint line is the population's average day - the baseline every cluster is measured against.

Population average day
04 · THE FEATURES

Fifty-one ways to describe a curve

A shape is more than 24 numbers. Alongside the hourly bins sit summary descriptors: the share of energy in each part of the day, how peaked the curve is, how much it varies hour to hour, its entropy and inequality, its dominant periodicities, and how different its weekend looks. Together, 51 behavioural features - and not one of them is the raw size of the consumer.

05 · THE METHOD

Compress, then group

Fifty-one features are correlated, so they are first standardised and passed through PCA, which keeps the 14 components that together hold 95% of the variance. K-Means then groups consumers in that compressed space, and the number of groups is chosen by a rule fixed in advance - not picked to look tidy.

06 · THE CLUSTERS

Three ways a day is spent

The rule settled on three groups. They are not big, medium and small - they are three different daily rhythms. Each card shows the cluster's own average day (bold) against the population (faint).

Cluster identity is always colour and name. The numbers below each name are the count of consumers, their share of the population, and the hour their average curve peaks.
07 · HONEST VALIDATION

Stable grouping, modest separation

Good practice is to report the awkward number as plainly as the flattering one. The clusters are highly reproducible across random restarts, and they line up moderately with the hidden archetypes. But the separation is modest - a silhouette of 0.31 means the groups touch at their edges rather than sitting far apart. Both things are true at once.

08 · WHAT WE LEARNED

Timing is a real axis - here

On this generated world, grouping by shape recovers timing patterns that pure size would miss, and it does so stably. It also shows the limit honestly: the groups overlap, and a single synthetic draw is not the world. The finding is about a method's behaviour on data built to test it - a narrower and more truthful claim than a statement about real homes.

The lineage

Where the method comes from

Grouping households by the shape of their load curve is an established idea. These peer-reviewed studies shaped the approach used here - shape-based features, PCA before clustering, and honest internal validation. This project is synthetic, so it does not reproduce their real-world findings; the papers are context and method lineage, not evidence for anything claimed on this page.
